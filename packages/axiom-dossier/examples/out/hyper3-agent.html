Abstract

The objective of HYPER-3 is to determine which of three daily doses should proceed into phase III and whether any dose harms the people taking it, targeting the effect of dose on sbp. Introduction

This report investigates which of three daily doses should proceed into phase III trials and whether any of the doses causes harm to the individuals receiving it. The primary analytical target is the effect of dose on systolic blood pressure. Identification of this target effect is obtained via the backdoor route. Answering the central research question requires evaluating the intent-to-treat effect of the assigned treatment arms. The assessment framework monitors whether any dose arm results in worse participant outcomes compared to standard care, both overall and within pre-specified age strata. Determining an appropriate dose involves characterizing the dose–response profile while maintaining sequential reviews to identify potential adverse effects. Because no explicit decision threshold was recorded in the trial specification, the analysis does not judge whether the magnitude of an effect is sufficient to warrant specific clinical action. Instead, the report provides the empirical point estimates alongside their corresponding Wald intervals. Presenting the identified quantities with their uncertainty intervals defines the statistical basis for evaluating dose selection and safety.

Methods

The trial evaluates three daily doses of an antihypertensive across four study arms over a six-month period with weekly assessments to identify candidate doses for phase III and monitor for harm. The analytical target is the effect of dose on sbp, which is identified via the backdoor route. The identification strategy relies on the assigned dose and avoids conditioning on post-randomization adherence, dropout, or subsequent blood pressure measurements. A total of 400 units are enrolled across the four arms to permit evaluation both across the pooled sample and within individual age strata. To model dose selection beyond the tested points, dose–response relationships are estimated by fitting a response surface with a saturating kernel per treatment. A sequential monitoring design is implemented to discontinue any dose arm that results in worse outcomes than standard care either overall or within any prespecified age band. Safety reviews are scheduled every second calendar week starting at week 12, once three eighths of the safety information is collected. The protocol specifies eight safety reviews assessing twelve contrasts at each look against predefined stopping boundaries. Arms are monitored at each look across age strata to enforce protocol-defined stopping rules prior to final readout.

Limitations

Identification of the effect of dose on systolic blood pressure relies on the backdoor route. This identification licenses the estimated effect under the assumption that the backdoor path remains properly blocked. Randomization licenses the effect of the assigned dose on the outcome specifically within the population that was randomized. That license holds only for analyses that do not condition on variables downstream of treatment assignment. Three factors in the study sit downstream of the assignment: adherence, dropout, and the pressure readings themselves. Conditioning on adherence would challenge the validity of the identification strategy. Adjusting for participant dropout would likewise compromise the licensed effect. Incorporating intermediate pressure readings into the analysis would also violate the conditions of the license. Any analytical model that conditions on these downstream variables threatens the standing assumptions. Furthermore, unobserved confounding that reopens the backdoor route would directly challenge the identification. The findings remain conditional on avoiding these post-assignment adjustments. Consequently, the identification holds only within the structural limits defined by the unconditioned assignment.
