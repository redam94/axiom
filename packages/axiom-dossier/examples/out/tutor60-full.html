TUTOR-60

Does buying fewer tutoring minutes for every eligible student beat buying the pilot's ninety weekly minutes for the fraction of them the budget reaches?

§Abstract

This study examined the allocation of tutoring minutes under a fixed budget for eligible students. The question asked was whether buying fewer tutoring minutes for every eligible student beats buying the pilot allocation's ninety weekly minutes for the fraction of them the budget reaches. This was answered by comparing cohort reading points over the pilot allocation against a decision threshold of zero points per year. The effect of tutoring on gain is identified via the backdoor route in the randomization design. The analysis found that cohort reading points over the pilot allocation settled above the threshold on the favourable side at 13838 points per year with a 90% HDI from 6163 to 22036 points per year. The value of the difference at the department's own valuation also settled above the threshold on the favourable side. These findings depend on the unverified assumption that the cohort average answers the question. The results indicate that spreading a lower dose across all eligible students delivers more total cohort reading points than funding a high dose for a smaller fraction of the students.

Reported quantities: Cohort reading points over the pilot allocation; Value of the difference at the department's own valuation

§1. Introduction

This report addresses whether buying fewer tutoring minutes for every eligible student beats buying the pilot's ninety weekly minutes for the fraction of them the budget reaches. The target quantity is identified. The question is answered by cohort reading points over the pilot allocation, evaluated against a decision threshold of 0 points/year. An interval that lies entirely on one side of 0 points/year settles the question. An interval strictly above 0 points/year indicates that allocating fewer minutes to every eligible student yields higher cohort reading points than the pilot allocation. An interval strictly below 0 points/year indicates that the pilot allocation yields higher cohort reading points. An interval spanning 0 points/year leaves the comparison unsettled.

§2. Methods

To address the question introduced earlier, the target is the effect of tutoring on gain, identified via the backdoor route. The graph consists of poverty_share -> gain, resources -> gain, resources -> poverty_share, and tutoring -> gain, where resources are unmeasured, as shown in Table 1 and Figure 1. The trial design parameters are shown in Table 3. Across that design, 120 schools were randomized to allocations drawn from a lattice over the two doses, with 3 messaging levels and tutoring spread across the decision range, leaving the adjustment set empty. Relying on state records on 240 schools was considered instead, but in that graph poverty_share -> gain, poverty_share -> tutoring, resources -> gain, resources -> poverty_share, resources -> tutoring, and tutoring -> gain, which downgrades the effect because resources are unmeasured. A two-arm trial at the pilot's 90 minutes was also considered and rejected because it would only answer whether tutoring works rather than the shape of the response. The recommendation is flat between 700 and 750 USD — 35 to 38 weekly minutes are all within two per cent of the best. The decision that matters is not the exact figure, it is not funding 90 minutes.

For the pilot to be the better buy, the advantage would have to be overstated by 100% of itself — that is, essentially the whole of it would have to be an artefact of the fit rather than a real difference between the two allocations.

The reason the recommendation is not 90 minutes is not that 90 minutes fails. It is that the response is steep at the bottom, the budget is fixed, and every extra dollar a student is a student not served.

§5. Model checking

Diagnostic checks evaluate the fit and can lower confidence in an estimate, but they can never establish one. The estimation relies on 4000 posterior draws across the 120 randomized schools. The posterior probability that the recommendation beats the pilot is 0.997. Under the recommended allocation, the intervention reaches 12000 students. By contrast, the pilot allocation would reach 4878 students. The cost of the recommended allocation is 745 USD per student. Recorded diagnostics are presented in Table 5. §6. Discussion

Both key findings agree and settle above the threshold on the favourable side. Specifically, the value of the difference at the department's own valuation and the cohort reading points over the pilot allocation each lie on the side favoring the broader distribution of tutoring minutes over the pilot allocation. Because the analysis relies on the backdoor route identified in the methods, these estimates can be read as effects of the allocation choice rather than baseline associations. That reading holds under the identification strategy but remains conditional on 2 unresolved assumptions. The limitations section states what each of those assumptions claims and what would challenge it.

§7. Conclusions

Buying fewer tutoring minutes for every eligible student beats buying the pilot's ninety weekly minutes for the fraction of students reached by the budget. Cohort reading points over the pilot allocation settle on the favourable side of the decision threshold at 13838 points/year (90% HDI 6163 to 22036 points/year). The value of the difference at the department's own valuation also settles on the favourable side of the threshold. This finding follows from the identification set out earlier in the report. The conclusion is conditional on 2 unresolved assumptions: a reading point has the value the state assigns it, and the cohort average answers the question. What each assumption claims is stated in the limitations section. The policy answer holds under these conditions.

§8. Limitations

Two unresolved assumptions support the analysis beyond what the data supply. First, the analysis asserts that a reading point has the value the state assigns it. Under this claim, a reading point is valued at 1,400 USD. This licenses the recommendation because the decision does not depend on this number, only on whether to fund at all. The assertion would be challenged by a valuation low enough to make the whole program not worth funding. Second, the analysis leaves unverified the assumption that the cohort average answers the question. This licenses basing the recommendation on the cohort average, as the grade-band breakdown the program office asked for is not estimable from a school-randomized trial. This assumption would be challenged by a response that differs across grade bands enough to change the allocation.
