HYPER-3: the trial that has to stop an arm

Adults with elevated blood pressure are randomized to standard care or to one of three daily doses of an investigational agent. Seated systolic pressure is measured weekly for {{casestudy.protocol.follow_up_weeks}} weeks. Everything below is computed with axiom against a synthetic world whose truth we know — which is the only way to check that a method catches what it is supposed to catch.

The protocol

{{casestudy.protocol.n_units}} patients, four arms, three age bands

Randomization is stratified by age band in 2:1:1:1 blocks, so the shared control arm is twice the size of each dose arm. Enrollment is staggered over {{casestudy.protocol.enrollment_weeks}} calendar weeks, which is what makes an interim review a real event: at any given review some patients have months of follow-up and some have weeks.

The truth the trial is trying to find

Benefit in the young cancels harm in the old

The agent lowers blood pressure through a saturating dose–response. It also has a superlinear pressor effect that is negligible below 20 mg and concentrated in the oldest band, where clearance is slowest. Below is what that does to the net benefit — and why looking only at the arm average is not a conservative choice.

Why the average is the dangerous statistic here

Follow the grey line to 40 mg. Pooled over the whole trial population the contrast is {{casestudy.truth.at_40.pooled_itt:+.2f}} mmHg — which is to say the top dose looks indistinguishable from doing nothing. Unremarkable, not alarming. A monitoring rule watching that number has nothing to fire on.

Now split it. At the same dose the youngest band is at {{casestudy.truth.at_40.by_stratum.age_25_35:+.2f}} — a substantial benefit — while the oldest is at {{casestudy.truth.at_40.by_stratum.age_51_plus:+.2f}}, a substantial rise in blood pressure. The average is near zero because the two are cancelling. Averaging is not the conservative choice here; it is the choice that destroys the only signal that matters.

Worth noting what the dose is doing to the pooled curve even so: at 20 mg the pooled contrast is {{casestudy.truth.at_20.pooled_itt:+.2f}} mmHg, and going up to 40 mg moves it to {{casestudy.truth.at_40.pooled_itt:+.2f}}. The higher dose is worse on average — but “worse than the middle dose” is not the same alarm as “harming a quarter of the patients”, and only one of those triggers a stopping rule.

The rule, written before the data

A boundary the statistic has to cross

The protocol commits in advance: stop an arm when there is at least a {{casestudy.rule.probability:.0%}} posterior probability that it is worse than control by more than {{casestudy.rule.margin:.0f}} mmHg. axiom turns that sentence into a Z threshold at every scheduled review, accounting for how much information has accumulated by then.

the rule, for one contrast 
information = information_fractions(enrolled_by_look, n_total)
schedule    = LookSchedule(labels=..., information=information)

rule = StoppingRule(
    name="harm|age_51_plus|dose_40",
    looks=schedule,
    boundaries=(harm_boundary(
        {{casestudy.rule.probability:.2f}},            # posterior probability that fires the rule
        information,
        margin={{casestudy.rule.margin:.1f}},        # mmHg worse than control
        se_at_full_information=se,
    ),),
)

path = monitor(rule, observed_z, effects=effects, ses=ses)

Two things are worth noticing. The boundary is derived from the protocol sentence rather than chosen from a table — change the margin or the probability and the thresholds move accordingly. And the same StoppingRule object is used both to check the realized data and to compute the rule’s operating characteristics before the trial opens, so the thing being evaluated is the thing that will actually run.

Stopping early costs you something, and axiom records what

The decision writes a ledger line: “{{casestudy.stop.ledger_statement}}”

The assumption it carries is {{casestudy.stop.assumption_name}} — {{casestudy.stop.assumption_statement}} It is challenged by: {{casestudy.stop.challenged_by}}

This matters more than it sounds. An arm stopped at a boundary crossing has a biased effect estimate by construction: you stopped precisely because the reading was extreme, so the reading overstates the effect. The right thing for a library to do is not to hide that, and not to silently correct it either, but to record it as an assumption attached to the number so that whoever writes the report knows a stage-wise-ordered estimate is owed.
