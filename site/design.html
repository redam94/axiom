Pillar 02 · axiom.design

What should I measure next?

Sample-size calculators answer a narrow question: how many units to detect an effect of a size you nominated. axiom asks the wider one. What do you already believe? What would the experiment actually change your mind about? What is that worth, what does withholding treatment cost, and after both — is this experiment worth running at all?

Step one

Start from what you already believe

A pilot has been run and fitted. Before choosing a size, axiom looks at the posterior for the contrast you care about and asks whether the conventional threshold is even in question.

What the pilot already says

5.59
3.73 7.43

90% equal-tailed · posterior sd 1.15 · the simulation’s truth is 3.54

Powering for the conventional threshold would waste the study

The pilot already puts 99.9% of posterior mass above 2.0. Designing a study to detect 2.0 would spend money confirming something you are already sure of. axiom anchors instead to the 4.19 at the sceptical end of what you believe — the effect that is genuinely still in play.

Step two

Then size it

With the effect to power for settled at 4.19 and an outcome sd of 4.55, the arithmetic is ordinary — and axiom will also tell you what a size someone has already proposed actually buys.

powered n to reach 80% power for an effect of 4.19 you need 38 units — 19 treated, 19 control 81.0%
proposed n someone proposed 80. That detects 2.85 at 80% power, and reaches 98.5% power for the effect in question over-powered
precision standard error of the difference at the powered size — this is the number every value calculation below runs on 1.478

Over-powering is not free. Every extra unit in the control arm is a unit not receiving a treatment you mostly believe works, and that cost shows up explicitly in the next section rather than being left as a footnote.

Step three

What is the answer worth?

An experiment is only worth running if it can change a decision. Here the programme scales up if the contrast clears 6.0, each outcome unit is worth 400 USD, and the programme covers 500 units. The prior sits just under the threshold with real spread — which is exactly when information has value.

Where the value comes from

The curve is steep at the right and flat at the left: buying precision is worth a great deal until the decision stops being in doubt, and almost nothing after. Had the threshold been 1.0 instead — comfortably below the prior — the whole curve would sit near zero, and the correct recommendation would be to run no experiment and act now.

Step four

Subtract what it costs to find out

Three candidate designs, scored on the same decision. Net value is the information they buy minus the treatment withheld from the control arm minus the fixed cost of running them.

The recommendation is stable

axiom re-runs the whole comparison over a grid of each economic input. Scaling the value per outcome across 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0× leaves the winner unchanged, and so does scaling the experiment’s standard error across 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0×. No tipping points were found in either sweep — the answer does not hinge on a number someone guessed.

The price is on the record

The value per outcome is not a hard-coded constant. It is a stated input that writes its own ledger line: “one unit is worth 400 USD (stated by the decision owner, 2026)” — so a reader six months later can see who claimed it and challenge that rather than the arithmetic.
