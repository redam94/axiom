Guide · doing the work

Folding an experiment in

You have an observational model of the whole system and one clean experimental measurement of a small corner of it. The experiment is far more trustworthy and far less complete. Neither on its own answers the decision. Calibration is how the trustworthy thing disciplines the complete one.

Why not just use the experiment

The experiment answers a smaller question than the decision asks

On the calibrate page, an experiment measures a first-period lift of 5.322 with a standard error of 0.053. That is a precise, randomized, defensible number.

The decision needs something else: the steady-state effect of a dose the experiment never tested, which is 32. The experiment measured one point on a curve; the decision asks about a different point on the same curve, after the dynamics have settled.

So the experiment cannot answer the question by itself, and the observational model — fitted on data where treatment and confounder correlate at 0.96 — cannot be trusted on its own either. Calibration is the operation that uses each for what it is good at.

This is a transfer, and it needs a licence

Moving from “first-period lift at the tested dose” to “steady-state effect at the decision dose” crosses the window and intervention facets. Every word of the transportability guide applies, and the corrections are recorded in a ledger rather than absorbed into a fitted parameter.

The measurement knows what it measured

A Measurement in axiom carries the estimand it is a measurement of — here first_period_lift@rct-2026Q1, with target hash 4760098b3929. That is what lets the transfer be checked rather than assumed.

The three routes

Where the experiment enters the model changes what it does

There is no single “incorporate the experiment” operation. There are three, they make different claims, and they give different answers on the same data.

As a prior

Turn the measurement into a prior on the relevant parameter and fit as usual. Simple and familiar — but it says the experiment constrains that parameter, when what it actually constrains is a derived quantity the parameter only partly controls.

Says: “my belief about this parameter is now centred here.”

As a likelihood term

Add the measurement to the likelihood as an observation of the derived quantity, computed through the same forward() the model uses. The experiment then constrains whatever combination of parameters actually produces it.

Says: “whatever the parameters are, they must reproduce this measurement.”

As a hard constraint

Require the model to match the measurement exactly. Rarely right — it treats a measurement with a standard error as though it had none, and it makes the experiment unfalsifiable by the rest of the data.

Says: “this is true, full stop.”

uncalibrated The observational fit alone. The parameter lands 2.66 standard deviations from the truth, and the realized decision quantity is 9.78 against a truth of 32. disagrees
prior route The measurement folded in as a prior on the amplitude. The parameter is now close to the truth — and the decision quantity still disagrees with the experiment, because pinning the parameter is not the same as reproducing the measurement. disagrees
likelihood route The measurement as a likelihood term on the derived quantity. The parameter is less precisely pinned, and that is the point: the model is free to find any parameters that reproduce the experiment, and the realized quantity lands at 5.47. agrees

The middle row is the instructive one. By the obvious diagnostic — is the parameter near its true value — the prior route looks best of the three. By the only diagnostic that matters — does the model agree with the experiment on the quantity the experiment measured — it fails. A parameter is not a quantity, and calibrating the first does not calibrate the second.

The check

Agreement, stated as a number

After calibrating, ask the model to predict the thing the experiment measured, and compare. That comparison is a z statistic against the measurement's own standard error, and it is the check that distinguishes the three routes above: -4.29, -2.82 and -0.64 respectively.

A model that cannot reproduce the experiment it was calibrated against has not been calibrated. It has been nudged, and the nudge went somewhere else in the parameter space.

When the model and the experiment genuinely disagree after an honest fold-in, that is information — usually about the transfer, not the experiment. The window was wrong, the population differed, or the dose the experiment tested is on a part of the curve the model has the wrong shape for.

Calibration is a loop, not a step

Fit, find what the decision is most sensitive to, measure that, fold it in, re-decide, and look again at what is now most uncertain. Each turn tells you what the next experiment should be. The depot tutorial runs one full turn, and the experiment reverses the decision the observational fit would have made.

Keep the ledger across the loop

Every fold-in writes lines — the pooling of measurements, the design factor, the transfer corrections. After three turns that ledger is the only account of why the current model believes what it believes. See provenance.
