Tutorials · 5 problems

Worked all the way through

Most library tours show you a subpackage at a time. These take a single problem and carry it through every phase it actually has to survive — because the hard part of causal work is not any one phase, it is that they have to agree with each other at the end.

Start with the porting one if you have a table and want to know what it takes to get it into axiom at all. It imports no simulator: every object you would have to write yourself is written on the page — the column roles, the treatment and outcome with their dimensions, the model spec, the estimand — and it ends where an honest porting exercise ends, with a number and a precise statement of the one thing standing between it and a causal claim.

Next to it, a model of your own, for when the kernels do not fit your mechanism. It writes the mean as an expression by hand, gets the dimensions checked before anything is fitted, and is honest about what leaving the kernels behind costs you.

The other two are the two shapes the work itself comes in. In one, the data already exists and the question is whether it can answer anything — it cannot, so an experiment gets designed, and its answer reverses the decision the existing data would have supported.

In the other there is no data at all. Every step is a decision about what to go and measure: which angles, what shape of aperture, how long at each, and when you are allowed to stop. It is the older discipline and the one that is easier to get wrong, because nothing pushes back until the money is spent.

A transfer story · 6 steps

Making a model travel

A household water-filter programme was evaluated in one district over twelve weeks, per household. The decision is in a different district, per person, over a year. What has to be true for the first number to be an answer to the second question — and what does it cost to assume it?

This is the failure mode that survives every other check. The identification was fine, the fit was fine, the interval was honest, and the number was still wrong where it landed, because nobody wrote down that the question had changed on the way. Eight facets, a selection diagram, four things a graph can say, and a ledger you could hand to somebody who has to sign for it.

A design story · 6 steps

Designing an experiment before there is any data

Is the positive charge of an atom concentrated in a small hard centre, or spread through the whole atom? Both models fit everything known in 1910. What experiment would tell them apart — and specifically, which scattering angles, what shape of aperture, and for how long?

Nothing here is an analysis. Every step decides what to go and measure, and the whole argument turns on noticing that the two rival atoms are one model at two values of one number. That is what gives the experiment a resolving power, a sensitivity curve, and a rule for when to stop.
