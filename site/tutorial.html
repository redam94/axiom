Tutorials · 2 problems

Worked all the way through

Most library tours show you a subpackage at a time. These take a single problem and carry it through every phase it actually has to survive — because the hard part of causal work is not any one phase, it is that they have to agree with each other at the end.

They are deliberately the two different shapes the work comes in. In the first, the data already exists and the question is whether it can answer anything — it cannot, so an experiment gets designed, and its answer reverses the decision the existing data would have supported.

In the second there is no data at all. Every step is a decision about what to go and measure: which angles, what shape of aperture, how long at each, and when you are allowed to stop. It is the older discipline and the one that is easier to get wrong, because nothing pushes back until the money is spent.

One question, carried all the way

A design story · 6 steps

Designing an experiment before there is any data

Is the positive charge of an atom concentrated in a small hard centre, or spread through the whole atom? Both models fit everything known in 1910. What experiment would tell them apart — and specifically, which scattering angles, what shape of aperture, and for how long?

Nothing here is an analysis. Every step decides what to go and measure, and the whole argument turns on noticing that the two rival atoms are one model at two values of one number. That is what gives the experiment a resolving power, a sensitivity curve, and a rule for when to stop.
